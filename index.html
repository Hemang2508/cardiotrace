The science

Electrical signals,
clinical meaning.

The electrocardiogram records the heart's electrical activity through surface electrodes. Each waveform — P, QRS, T — reflects a discrete electrophysiological event. Deviations from normal morphology carry diagnostic weight that can alter the entire clinical course.

P

Atrial depolarisation

Coordinated electrical activation across both atria from the sinoatrial node. Duration 80–120 ms; amplitude < 0.25 mV.

QRS

Ventricular depolarisation

Rapid depolarisation of both ventricles. Normal duration < 120 ms. Widening indicates conduction pathology or accessory pathway conduction.

T

Ventricular repolarisation

T wave reflects ventricular recovery. Inversion or prolonged QT may indicate ischaemia, electrolyte disturbance, or channelopathy.

Methodology

How the simulation works.

Each waveform is mathematically synthesised using Gaussian kernel superposition, rhythm-specific beat timing, and physiologically calibrated parameters from cardiology literature.

1

Gaussian Synthesis

Individual waveform components (P, Q, R, S, T) are modelled as Gaussian functions with clinically accurate amplitude and timing for each condition.

2

Signal Composition

Components are superimposed at physiologically correct intervals — RR spacing, PR duration, and QRS width — reflecting the specific arrhythmia.

3

Rhythm Modelling

Beats are assembled with condition-specific timing — irregular RR intervals in AF, dropped QRS complexes in Mobitz II — and extra features such as fibrillatory waves, flutter waves, and the WPW delta wave are added where the condition requires them.

4

Noise Injection

Four additive noise sources — white Gaussian noise at a set SNR, respiratory baseline wander, occasional motion-artefact bursts, and 50 Hz powerline interference — replicate real-world recording conditions.
